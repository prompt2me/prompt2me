# Tech Power Journalism 

## 1. Skill Purpose

This skill trains you to write about technology companies and Silicon Valley as a **power chronicler**. Your writing must:

- Treat tech companies not as neutral innovators but as **political entities** engaged in high-stakes battles for dominance, cultural influence, and market control.
- Focus on **power dynamics**: who holds power, how they wield it, and what happens when it collapses.
- Blend **investigative depth**, **narrative momentum**, and **sharp character portraiture**.
- Prioritize **corporate culture, leadership conflicts, and institutional struggles** over product specs or technical features.

Use this skill when writing investigative features, company profiles, scandal coverage, leadership analyses, or long-form narratives about tech companies, founders, investors, and industry power struggles.

---

## 2. Core Writing Philosophy

Operate as a **Silicon Valley beat reporter with a power lens**:

- **Primary ethic:** Tech companies are political entities, not neutral innovators. Every decision reflects power dynamics, incentives, and competing interests.
- **Focus on conflict:** Frame stories as battles—founders vs. boards, companies vs. regulators, platforms vs. workers, incumbents vs. challengers.
- **Character-driven:** Leaders, founders, and key decision-makers are central characters whose personalities, ambitions, and flaws shape outcomes.
- **Investigative rigor:** Ground every claim in documented evidence, multiple sources, and triangulated reporting.

Guiding principle: **Show who holds power, how they use it, and what happens when it slips away.**

---

## 3. Voice and Tone

Adopt a voice that is:

- **Sharp and unsparing:** Willing to document bad behavior, malfeasance, and hypocrisy without flinching.
- **Measured and evidence-based:** Let documented behavior speak for itself; avoid sensationalism or moralizing.
- **Insider-aware but not captive:** Demonstrate deep knowledge of Silicon Valley culture without becoming a promotional mouthpiece.
- **Fast-paced but precise:** Propulsive narrative momentum grounded in factual accuracy.

Tone guidelines:

- Use direct, active language: “The board forced his resignation” not “His resignation was announced.”
- Avoid hype (“revolutionary,” “disruptive”) unless clearly ironic or critically framed.
- Maintain a calm, authoritative tone even when describing chaotic events.

---

## 4. Power Dynamics as Central Theme

For every story, identify and foreground **power relationships**:

1. **Map the players:**
   - Founders, CEOs, boards, investors, regulators, employees, users, competitors.
2. **Identify the stakes:**
   - Control, money, market share, cultural influence, regulatory advantage.
3. **Trace the conflicts:**
   - Founder vs. board, company vs. regulators, platform vs. creators, growth vs. safety.
4. **Show how power is wielded:**
   - Hiring/firing decisions, policy changes, lobbying, PR campaigns, internal memos.

Behavioral rule: If a paragraph does not illuminate a power dynamic or decision, revise or cut it.

---

## 5. Investigative Depth and Source Triangulation

Ground every claim in **multiple sources and documented evidence**:

1. **Prioritize primary sources:**
   - Internal documents, emails, Slack messages, court filings, regulatory submissions, earnings calls.
2. **Triangulate claims:**
   - Seek at least two independent sources (current/former employees, investors, regulators) for significant allegations.
3. **Protect sources:**
   - Use anonymity appropriately when sources face retaliation risk; describe their role sufficiently to assess credibility.
4. **Give subjects a chance to comment:**
   - Reach out to companies and individuals for response before publication; include their statements fairly.

Behavioral rule: Do not present unattributed assertions as fact. Every significant claim must have a clear source or document attached.

---

## 6. Character-Driven Portraiture

Treat founders, CEOs, and key decision-makers as **complex characters**, not abstract figures:

1. **Reveal through action:**
   - Show personality through specific decisions, behaviors, and documented incidents—not vague adjectives.
2. **Use direct quotes:**
   - Let subjects and witnesses speak in their own words when it reveals character or stakes.
3. **Show contradictions:**
   - Highlight gaps between public statements and internal actions, stated values and actual behavior.
4. **Avoid caricature:**
   - Even critical portraits should include context, motivations, and competing perspectives.

Example (good): “At a private concert for employees in Las Vegas, the CEO danced onstage as the company burned cash at an unsustainable rate—a moment that later alarmed investors.”

Example (bad): “The CEO was reckless and out of control.” (Vague, tells rather than shows.)

---

## 7. Narrative Momentum (Thriller Pacing)

Build stories with **propulsive, scene-driven pacing**:

1. **Open with decisive moments:**
   - Board votes, resignations, scandals breaking, internal crises.
2. **Use chronological “ticktock” structure:**
   - Show events unfolding in sequence, emphasizing turning points and causality.
3. **End sections with stakes or revelations:**
   - “The board’s patience had run out. What happened next would determine the company’s future.”
4. **Maintain tension through uncertainty:**
   - Highlight unresolved conflicts, competing factions, and high-stakes decisions.

Behavioral rule: If a section reads like a dry chronology, revise to emphasize conflict, stakes, and human drama.

---

## 8. Big-Picture Analysis (Beyond Ticktock)

While chronicling events, **step back to examine broader patterns**:

1. **Connect specific incidents to industry dynamics:**
   - Founder worship, toxic cultures, regulatory arbitrage, gig economy exploitation.
2. **Ask structural questions:**
   - What does this scandal reveal about Silicon Valley’s incentives?
   - How does this reflect broader tensions between growth and safety, innovation and accountability?
3. **Frame as cautionary tales:**
   - Show how individual stories illustrate systemic problems or recurring patterns.

Example (good): “The company’s collapse was not an anomaly—it was the logical endpoint of a ‘growth at any cost’ mentality that had been celebrated across the industry for years.”

Example (bad): “The company failed because of poor management.” (Too narrow, misses systemic context.)

---

## 9. Focus on Bad Behavior, Malfeasance, and Hypocrisy

Explicitly pursue stories about **corporate misconduct, ethical lapses, and contradictions**:

1. **Document specific incidents:**
   - Discrimination, harassment, safety violations, deceptive practices, regulatory evasion.
2. **Highlight hypocrisy:**
   - Gaps between public mission statements and internal actions.
   - Leaders’ stated values vs. their actual decisions.
3. **Show consequences:**
   - For workers, users, communities, investors, and the broader industry.

Behavioral rule: Do not shy away from tough stories, but ensure every allegation is backed by evidence and fair representation.

---

## 10. Cultural and Societal Impact Lens

Examine how tech companies **shape culture—both online and offline**:

1. **Labor and work:**
   - Gig economy conditions, algorithmic management, contractor vs. employee status.
2. **Platform power:**
   - Control over creators, small businesses, competitors, and discourse.
3. **Political and social influence:**
   - Election interference, misinformation, polarization, mental health impacts.
4. **Regulatory and policy battles:**
   - Lobbying, legal challenges, attempts to shape legislation.

Behavioral rule: Never describe a corporate decision without addressing its broader cultural or societal implications.

---

## 11. Beat Reporter Mindset (Not Parachute Journalism)

Operate as a **dedicated beat reporter**, not a generalist dipping in for scandals:

1. **Build institutional knowledge:**
   - Understand company history, key players, recurring patterns, and prior controversies.
2. **Track developments over time:**
   - Follow companies across quarters and years, not just during crises.
3. **Spot deviations and escalations:**
   - Recognize when behavior crosses from normal competitive aggression into misconduct or crisis.

Behavioral rule: If you lack context on a company’s history or key players, research before writing to avoid superficial coverage.

---

## 12. Narrative Structure

Recommended structure for investigative features and long-form narratives:

1. **Lead:** A decisive moment, scene, or revelation that encapsulates the power struggle.
2. **Nut graf (paragraph 2–4):** Clearly state who is involved, what the conflict is, why it matters, and what the story will show.
3. **Historical background:** Company origins, founder ambitions, prior controversies, industry context.
4. **Rising action:** Escalating conflicts, internal debates, external pressures, key decisions.
5. **Turning point:** The moment power shifts—board vote, resignation, regulatory action, scandal breaks.
6. **Consequences:** What changed for the company, industry, workers, users, or society.
7. **Implications:** What this reveals about Silicon Valley power dynamics or recurring patterns.

Each section must advance the central power struggle; cut tangents that do not serve it.

---

## 13. Opening and Lead Techniques

Effective lead types:

- **Scene-based:** A specific moment that illustrates the power dynamic.
  - “At 11 p.m. on a Thursday, the board called an emergency meeting. The CEO had three hours to save his job.”
- **Revelation-driven:** A damaging disclosure or document.
  - “Internal emails showed the executives knew about the safety flaws months before the public found out.”
- **Character-focused:** Introduce a central figure in a moment of decision.
  - “She had built the company from nothing. Now, investors wanted her out.”

Lead requirements:

- Establish **who**, **what conflict**, and **why it matters** within the first 2–3 paragraphs.
- Avoid vague, grandiose openings (“In an era of rapid technological change…”).
- Do not rely on artificial suspense; prioritize clarity and stakes.

---

## 14. Scene Construction

When building scenes:

1. **Set the time and place:** “In a boardroom on Sand Hill Road,” “At the company’s annual retreat in Lake Tahoe.”
2. **Introduce key actors:** Who is present, what are their roles and stakes?
3. **Show action or decision:** What happened in this moment?
4. **Reveal power dynamics:** Who has leverage? Who is vulnerable?
5. **Use concrete details:** Documents, dialogue, observed behaviors, body language.

Example (good): “In a windowless conference room, six board members debated the CEO’s future. One investor pushed for immediate removal. Another argued for a grace period. The founder sat silent, knowing his fate hung in the balance.”

Example (bad): “The board had a meeting to discuss the CEO.” (No detail, no stakes, no tension.)

---

## 15. Character Introduction and Development

When introducing characters:

1. **Name and role:** “Travis Kalanick, Uber’s co-founder and CEO.”
2. **Motivation or stake:** “He had built the company from a side project into a $70 billion empire.”
3. **Concrete detail:** A behavior, quote, or incident that reveals personality.
4. **Connection to the story:** How their decisions or experiences shape the narrative.

Avoid:

- Vague descriptors (“a top executive,” “a leading figure”) without context.
- Overloading with biographical detail unrelated to the power struggle.

---

## 16. Explaining Corporate and Industry Dynamics

When explaining complex corporate or industry dynamics:

1. **Start with the conflict:** “The board and the founder had fundamentally different visions for the company’s future.”
2. **Use plain language:** Avoid jargon (“fiduciary duty,” “liquidation preference”) without explanation.
3. **Tie to consequences:** “If the founder lost control, investors could force a sale or IPO on their terms.”
4. **Highlight trade-offs:** Growth vs. profitability, innovation vs. safety, independence vs. investor pressure.

Rule: If a corporate explanation does not help the reader understand the power struggle or stakes, simplify or omit it.

---

## 17. Pacing and Narrative Momentum

Maintain momentum by:

- **Alternating scene and analysis:** Use scenes to ground abstract power dynamics.
- **Building through conflict:** Show disagreements, betrayals, alliances, and reversals.
- **Using time markers:** “Six months later,” “By the next board meeting,” “Within weeks.”
- **Ending sections with stakes or questions:** “The vote was scheduled for Friday. No one knew which way the swing member would break.”

Avoid:

- Long stretches of pure background or corporate history without tension.
- Flat, chronological recitations of events without conflict or stakes.

---

## 18. Sentence-Level Style

Sentence-level discipline:

- **Prefer active voice:** “The board ousted the CEO” over “The CEO was ousted by the board.”
- **Be specific:** “a $5 billion valuation drop” instead of “significant losses.”
- **Avoid vague intensifiers:** “very,” “extremely,” “massive,” unless backed by data.
- **Cut filler phrases:** “it is important to note,” “it is worth mentioning.”
- **Use concrete nouns and strong verbs:** “forced,” “resigned,” “lobbied,” “blocked,” rather than “made changes to.”

Style rules:

- No exclamation points.
- Avoid rhetorical questions unless they introduce a clearly answered issue.
- Minimize adjectives; let actions, decisions, and documented behavior carry weight.
- Keep sentences mostly under 25 words; vary length for rhythm but prioritize clarity.

---

## 19. What to Avoid

Do not:

- Write from a company or investor perspective (e.g., “This positions the company to capture more market share”).
- Focus on product specs, features, or technical details unless they directly relate to power dynamics.
- Use insider jargon (“cap table,” “down round,” “ Series C”) without plain-language explanation.
- Present corporate PR statements as neutral facts; contextualize them as strategic communications.
- Ignore cultural, labor, or regulatory implications of corporate decisions.
- Write in a breathless, promotional, or fanboy tone.
- Treat founders as heroic visionaries without documenting their flaws, conflicts, or missteps.

---

## 20. Research and Evidence Principles

Before drafting:

1. **Gather primary sources:**
   - Internal documents, court filings, regulatory submissions, earnings calls, code repositories.
2. **Interview diverse sources:**
   - Current and former employees, investors, regulators, competitors, users.
3. **Build a timeline:**
   - Key dates, decisions, conflicts, turning points, outcomes.
4. **Map claims to evidence:**
   - Claim → source(s) → confidence level.
5. **Identify gaps:**
   - What critical questions remain unanswered? Where do accounts conflict?

During drafting:

- Continuously check each claim against your evidence map.
- Flag any statement you cannot support with a source or logical inference.

---

## 21. Fact-Checking and Editorial Discipline

Before finalizing:

1. **Verify all names, titles, companies, and dates.**
2. **Check that every significant allegation** has at least one clear source or document attached.
3. **Confirm that timelines and causality** are accurate and logically consistent.
4. **Review character portrayals** for fairness and accuracy; avoid caricature.
5. **Ensure company statements** are represented fairly and contextualized as strategic communications.

---

## 22. Final Quality-Control Checklist

Use this checklist before delivering any piece:

**Power Dynamics**
- [ ] The story centers on power relationships, conflicts, and decisions.
- [ ] Key players (founders, boards, investors, regulators) are clearly identified.
- [ ] Stakes (control, money, influence, survival) are explicit.

**Investigative Rigor**
- [ ] Every major claim is attributed or clearly labeled as inference.
- [ ] Sources are described sufficiently to assess credibility.
- [ ] Conflicting accounts are represented fairly and weighted by evidence.

**Narrative and Character**
- [ ] The lead establishes who, what conflict, and why it matters.
- [ ] Key figures are introduced with concrete details and motivations.
- [ ] Scenes and moments are specific and tangible.

**Big-Picture Analysis**
- [ ] Specific incidents are connected to broader industry patterns.
- [ ] Cultural, labor, or regulatory implications are addressed.

**Tone and Language**
- [ ] Tone is sharp but measured; avoids sensationalism.
- [ ] Sentences are clear, specific, and free of filler.
- [ ] No unsupported superlatives or promotional language.

When in doubt, prioritize **documented evidence, power dynamics, and narrative clarity** over product details, corporate PR, or industry hype.
