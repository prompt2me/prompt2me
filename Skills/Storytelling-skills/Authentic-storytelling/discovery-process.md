# Story Discovery Process

Do not write the story before understanding the underlying reality. Work through these steps in order — each one narrows toward the material that actually makes a story worth telling. If the user hasn't given you enough to answer a step, ask rather than inventing a plausible answer.

## Step 1 — Identify Brand Reality

Determine: What does the organization actually do? Who does it serve? Why does it exist? What problem does it solve? What does it believe? What does it refuse to compromise on? What makes it different? What experiences shaped it? What does it repeatedly do? What evidence supports its claims?

## Step 2 — Find the Origin

Investigate: What existed before the organization? What problem became difficult to ignore? What experience triggered action? Who recognized the problem? What decision was made? What alternatives existed? What risks were involved? What happened next?

Do not force the origin into a dramatic "one magical moment" narrative unless the evidence actually supports it. Most origins are messier and more gradual than the mythologized version — preserve that if that's what's true.

## Step 3 — Find the Tension

Identify the *real* tension in the story. Common patterns: old way vs. better way; convenience vs. quality; growth vs. principles; speed vs. craftsmanship; scale vs. personalization; assumptions vs. evidence; customer expectations vs. reality; problem vs. attempted solution.

Never manufacture conflict where none existed. Find the real tension — if the evidence shows the path was relatively smooth, say so; a smooth path can still be an interesting or credible story.

## Step 4 — Find the Turning Point

Identify moments when something changed: a customer revealed an unexpected problem; a prototype failed; an assumption proved wrong; a product decision changed; a better approach was discovered; a difficult decision clarified a principle; a customer problem revealed a larger opportunity.

The turning point must be grounded in evidence, not narrative convenience.

## Step 5 — Find the Human Stakes

Ask: *Why did this matter to real people?* Consider customers, founders, employees, communities, partners, and users. Translate abstract business language ("we improved efficiency") into concrete human consequences ("the ops team stopped staying late every Thursday to fix the same recurring error").

## Step 6 — Identify Values in Action

Do not merely list values. Find moments where values influenced actual behavior.

Instead of: *"We value craftsmanship."*
Look for evidence such as: *"The team rejected the first production batch because it didn't meet the established quality standard."*

Values become storytelling material when they influence decisions — not when they're stated as adjectives.

## Step 7 — Identify the Cost of the Value

Ask: *What has the brand refused to do because of what it believes?* Look for rejected shortcuts, difficult decisions, delayed launches, higher quality standards, lost opportunities, long-term investments, difficult promises kept, and short-term sacrifices.

Values become credible when they have consequences. A value that never cost the organization anything is much harder to make believable.

## Step 8 — Identify the Distinctive Truth

Ask: *What could another brand not truthfully say in exactly the same way?*

Avoid generic claims a hundred competitors could also make: "We care deeply," "We believe in innovation," "We put customers first," "We want to change the world," "We never settle." If the material you have only supports generic claims like these, keep digging for the specific evidence behind the claim rather than using the generic version.

---

By the end of this process you should be able to name: the real tension, the real turning point, the real human stakes, at least one value demonstrated through a costly decision, and the distinctive truth that differentiates this brand's story from a competitor's. If you can't name one of these from the material available, that's a gap to flag to the user rather than paper over.
