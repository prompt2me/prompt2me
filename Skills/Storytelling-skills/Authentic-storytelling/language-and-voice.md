# Language, Voice, and Structure

## Authentic Story Architecture

Use this 10-beat structure when it fits the material — don't force every story into every beat; use only the elements the evidence supports.

1. **Context** — What was happening?
2. **Tension** — What was not working?
3. **Recognition** — What did someone notice?
4. **Decision** — What did they choose?
5. **Cost** — What did the choice require?
6. **Action** — What happened next?
7. **Consequence** — What changed?
8. **Meaning** — What did the experience reveal?
9. **Identity** — What does it reveal about the brand?
10. **Present Relevance** — Why does it still matter?

## Evidence-to-Story Transformation (worked example)

This shows how to move from a raw fact to narrative meaning without changing what actually happened:

- **Raw fact:** "The company changed its packaging."
- **Context:** "Customers had become increasingly concerned about unnecessary packaging."
- **Decision:** "The company redesigned the packaging instead of simply adding a sustainability message to its marketing."
- **Tension:** "The new packaging increased production complexity."
- **Value in Action:** "The company accepted the additional complexity because reducing unnecessary material mattered more than maintaining the previous process."
- **Meaning:** "Sustainability became a design constraint rather than a marketing claim."

The principle this illustrates: authentic storytelling turns evidence into meaning without changing the underlying truth. Every added layer (context, tension, meaning) is an interpretation of the same fact, not a new invented fact.

## Language Principles

**Prefer specificity.**
- Weak: "We faced many challenges."
- Strong: "The first version failed during testing."

**Prefer scenes over claims.**
- Weak: "Our team cares about customers."
- Strong: "After three customers reported the same problem, the product team stopped the planned release and rebuilt the workflow."

**Prefer concrete details** — places, objects, decisions, actions, problems, consequences, relevant dates, specific examples. Specificity is what makes a claim checkable, and checkable claims are what make a story credible.

## Emotional Writing Rules

Do not tell audiences what to feel before giving them reasons to feel it. Avoid unsupported statements like "Everyone was incredibly frustrated." Prefer observable circumstances instead: "The team had been manually correcting the same errors every week." Let the audience infer the emotion from the situation — asserted emotion reads as manipulation; earned emotion reads as truth.

## Voice Principles

Authentic brand storytelling should be: confident without exaggeration, human without forced intimacy, emotional without manipulation, clear without oversimplification, distinctive without theatricality, humble without self-deprecation, persuasive without sounding artificial.

Avoid: corporate clichés, inspirational filler, generic purpose language, manufactured vulnerability, excessive superlatives, artificial drama, generic hero narratives, empty claims.

## Anti-Authenticity Filter

These phrases aren't automatically prohibited, but they're commonly used to *perform* authenticity rather than demonstrate it — flag them and require concrete evidence before using them, or replace the abstract declaration with the concrete behavior behind it:

- "We've always believed…"
- "Our journey began…"
- "Against all odds…"
- "We never gave up…"
- "It was more than a business…"
- "We wanted to change the world…"
- "We listened to our customers…"
- "We are passionate about…"
- "We are committed to…"
- "We believe anything is possible…"

Whenever one of these (or a phrase like it) shows up in a draft, ask: what specific, checkable thing happened that this phrase is standing in for? Replace the phrase with that thing.

## Authenticity vs. Vulnerability

Authenticity does not require exposing everything. Don't confuse honesty with oversharing, vulnerability with confession, transparency with indiscriminate disclosure, or imperfection with incompetence. A brand can remain fully authentic while maintaining privacy, confidentiality, professional boundaries, legal constraints, strategic discretion, and customer confidentiality. The operative question is: *what truthful information creates meaningful understanding?* — not "what's the most exposed a brand can be."

## Narrative Compression

Authenticity does not require telling the entire history. Preserve: meaningful context, essential tension, consequential decisions, revealing details, meaningful consequences, and strategic meaning. Remove: repetitive chronology, irrelevant milestones, corporate jargon, decorative adjectives, unsupported interpretation, and unnecessary background. The goal is maximum meaning per detail.
