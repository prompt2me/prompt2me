# Story Modes and Frameworks

Choose the storytelling mode that fits what the evidence actually supports and what the user needs — don't force every request into the same narrative shape.

## Story Modes

- **Origin Story** — Why did the organization begin?
- **Founder Story** — What human experience shaped the founder and organization?
- **Belief Story** — What does the organization genuinely believe?
- **Transformation Story** — What changed?
- **Conflict Story** — What meaningful problem had to be solved?
- **Discovery Story** — What did the organization learn?
- **Craft Story** — How is something made differently?
- **Customer Story** — How did a real customer's situation change?
- **Principle Story** — What experience created a lasting principle?
- **Culture Story** — What behaviors reveal how the organization works?
- **Purpose Story** — Why does the organization believe its work matters?

Do not use the same narrative structure for every mode — a Craft Story lives in process detail, a Founder Story lives in interior change, a Customer Story lives in a real before/after.

---

## Founder Story Framework

Investigate, in order:
- **Before** — Who was the founder before the organization existed?
- **Encounter** — What did the founder encounter?
- **Friction** — What problem became significant?
- **Insight** — What did the founder understand differently?
- **Experiment** — What did the founder try?
- **Resistance** — What didn't work?
- **Commitment** — Why did the founder continue?
- **Transformation** — What changed?
- **Present** — How does the original insight influence the organization today?

Do not automatically portray the founder as someone who knew the answer from the beginning. Preserve genuine uncertainty, false starts, and mistakes when the evidence supports them — a founder who "just knew" reads as mythology, not history.

---

## Company Origin Story Framework

Structure around: the world as it was → the problem → the people affected → the discovery → the decision → the first attempt → the obstacle → the adaptation → the result → the principle that emerged.

---

## Product Story Framework

Explain: *Why did this product need to exist?*

Investigate: the original problem, existing alternatives, customer insight, design constraints, rejected solutions, product decisions, difficult tradeoffs, unexpected discoveries, product philosophy, and consequences.

Connect product features to real human problems — a feature list is not a story; the decision behind the feature is.

---

## Customer Story Framework

Capture: situation before, problem, desired outcome, decision, experience, obstacles, change, measurable result, and human significance.

Never manufacture customer quotes, exaggerate outcomes, or generalize one customer's experience into a universal claim without evidence to support the generalization.

---

## Mission Story Framework

Connect the chain: **Mission → Principle → Decision → Behavior → Consequence.**

A mission becomes believable when it can be traced through to an actual decision and its consequence — not when it's simply restated in different words.

---

## Values Story Framework

For every stated value, ask:
1. What does this value mean in practice?
2. When has it influenced a decision?
3. What behavior demonstrates it?
4. What has the organization sacrificed for it?
5. What would the organization refuse to do because of it?
6. What evidence demonstrates that it is real?

If evidence doesn't exist for a value, label it as aspirational in your notes to the user rather than presenting it as demonstrated reality in the story.
