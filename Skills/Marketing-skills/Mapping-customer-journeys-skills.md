# Mapping Customer Journeys

## Purpose

Connect audience needs and jobs-to-be-done to the complete customer journey so marketing teams can deliver the right message, content, proof, and experience at each stage.

This Skill produces:

1. A practical **Journey Map**.
2. A prioritized **Content Needs Plan**.
3. Recommended touchpoints, messages, CTAs, and proof.
4. Gaps, friction points, and improvement opportunities.
5. Handoffs for strategy, content, campaigns, channels, and optimization Skills.

## Core principles

### Map the customer's progress

- Begin with the customer's situation, not the company's content calendar.
- Describe what the customer is trying to accomplish at each stage.
- Connect customer needs to messages, experiences, and actions.
- Distinguish the customer journey from the internal marketing funnel.
- Recognize that customers may skip, repeat, or move backward between stages.
- Include post-purchase experience when retention, expansion, or advocacy matters.

### Use evidence

- Separate observed behavior from assumptions.
- Do not invent customer emotions, questions, or touchpoints.
- Label unsupported conclusions as hypotheses.
- Cite supplied evidence where possible.
- Identify where additional research or measurement is needed.
- Treat funnel stages as planning models, not guaranteed customer behavior.

### Focus on decisions

Every journey stage should answer:

- What does the customer need now?
- What question are they asking?
- What is preventing progress?
- What experience or content would help?
- What proof is required?
- What action should the customer take?
- What should the business measure?

### Avoid content-first planning

Recommend content only when it helps the audience:

- Understand a problem.
- Recognize a need.
- Compare alternatives.
- Build trust.
- Resolve an objection.
- Take the next step.
- Succeed after purchase.
- Share or advocate.

## Required inputs

Use available information from: Business Context Brief, Brand Profile, Audience Profiles, Personas, Jobs-to-be-done, Customer research, Product and offer information, Competitor research, Existing funnel or lifecycle data, Website and analytics data, Campaign briefs, Content inventory, Channel information, Sales and customer-support information, Retention or churn data.

If important inputs are unavailable:

- Ask focused questions.
- Create a provisional map.
- Mark assumptions clearly.
- Identify the evidence needed to validate them.

---

## Journey model

Use the following stages when appropriate:

1. **Unaware or latent need**
2. **Awareness**
3. **Problem recognition**
4. **Exploration**
5. **Consideration**
6. **Evaluation**
7. **Conversion or purchase**
8. **Onboarding**
9. **Adoption or use**
10. **Retention or expansion**
11. **Advocacy**

Select stages based on business model, product complexity, sales cycle, customer behavior, marketing objective, and available evidence. For simple purchases, combine stages. For complex B2B or high-consideration offers, expand stages where decision friction is significant.

## Required workflow

### Step 1: Define the mapping objective

Identify the purpose of the journey map (e.g., acquire new customers, improve conversion, reduce churn, increase referrals, plan content, diagnose a funnel). State the primary decision the map should support.

### Step 2: Define the audience scope

Specify: Audience or segment, Persona (if relevant), Market or geography, Product or offer, Customer type, Buying situation, Relevant funnel or lifecycle, Primary objective. Do not create one generic journey for multiple audiences when their needs or buying processes differ materially.

### Step 3: Define the starting situation

Document: What triggers the journey, what the customer already knows, what problem or opportunity exists, what happens if the customer does nothing, what alternatives are currently used, what level of urgency exists, what event may cause the customer to seek help.

### Step 4: Define customer stages

For each stage, document: Customer goal, Customer question, Customer need, Job-to-be-done, Pain point, Motivation, Emotional state, Information requirement, Decision barrier, Desired progress, Success signal. Use customer-centered language.

### Step 5: Map touchpoints

Identify touchpoints across: Search, Social media, Video, Website, Landing pages, Email, Advertising, Communities, Reviews, Sales conversations, Product demonstrations, Webinars, Documentation, Checkout, Onboarding, Customer support, Product experience, Account management, Referral or advocacy channels.

For each touchpoint, explain: What the customer is trying to do, What the business currently provides, What the customer experiences, What gap or friction exists, Who owns the touchpoint. Do not assume that every touchpoint is controlled by marketing.

### Step 6: Map content needs

For every stage, identify content that helps the customer progress. For each content need, define: Customer question, Content objective, Recommended format, Core message, Required proof, CTA, Distribution channel, Owner, Priority, Measurement method.

### Step 7: Map messages and proof

Connect each stage to: Relevant positioning, Messaging pillar, Value proposition, Product benefit, Proof point, Objection response, Call to action. Do not use conversion-focused language in early stages when the audience first needs education or problem recognition.

### Step 8: Map emotions and friction

For every relevant stage, identify: Likely emotional state, Confidence level, Perceived risk, Effort required, Potential confusion, Trust concern, Reason for delay, Reason for abandonment, Friction reduction opportunity. Label emotions as evidence-based, inferred, or hypothetical.

### Step 9: Identify journey gaps

Look for: Stages with no relevant content, Repeated customer questions, Missing proof, Weak message transitions, Unclear CTAs, Poor message match between ad and landing page, Overly aggressive conversion requests, Conflicting information across touchpoints, Missing onboarding support, No retention or advocacy path, Measurement gaps, Ownership gaps.

### Step 10: Prioritize improvements

Rank opportunities using customer impact, business impact, journey friction, audience reach, evidence strength, implementation effort, and strategic alignment.

Priority scale:
- **Critical:** Blocks progress, creates serious risk, or affects a major business objective.
- **High:** Strongly limits movement through the journey.
- **Medium:** Creates avoidable friction or weakens clarity.
- **Low:** Useful refinement with limited immediate impact.

### Step 11: Define measurement

Choose metrics appropriate to each stage: Reach, Qualified reach, Engagement quality, Search visibility, Content completion, Repeat visits, Email engagement, Lead quality, Demo requests, Conversion rate, Sales-cycle duration, Assisted conversions, Activation, Product adoption, Retention, Expansion, Referral, Customer satisfaction. Do not treat engagement as success unless it connects to the journey objective.

### Step 12: Validate the map

Before finalizing, check that every stage has a customer goal, needs are connected to content or experience, CTAs match customer readiness, proof matches perceived risk, touchpoints reflect the actual customer process, and that current-state observations are separated from future recommendations. Identify assumptions and research gaps. Confirm metrics can actually be measured.

## Current-state and future-state rules

**Current-state map:** Describe what customers and the business currently do. Use evidence from existing behavior and touchpoints. Do not present recommendations as current facts. Identify known gaps and uncertainty.

**Future-state map:** Label recommendations as proposed. Explain the expected customer benefit. Identify required resources and dependencies. Define success metrics. Separate quick wins from structural changes.

---

## Special journey considerations

### B2B journeys

Separate the following roles when relevant: User, Champion, Economic buyer, Decision-maker, Procurement, Legal or compliance reviewer, Technical evaluator, Executive sponsor. Map their different questions, objections, evidence needs, and touchpoints.

### B2C journeys

Consider: Immediate need, Emotional context, Price sensitivity, Convenience, Trust, Social proof, Repeat purchase, Post-purchase support.

### SaaS or subscription journeys

Include: Signup, Activation, First value, Adoption, Habit formation, Renewal, Expansion, Churn risk, Advocacy.

### Creator or content-led journeys

Include: Discovery, Content consumption, Trust formation, Subscription or follow, Community participation, Lead capture, Product or service conversion, Referral.

### High-risk or regulated purchases

Include: Evidence requirements, Review and approval stages, Legal questions, Risk reduction, Human consultation, Required disclosures, Post-purchase support.

---

## Handling nonlinear journeys

Customers may skip stages, repeat stages, compare alternatives multiple times, return after abandoning, enter through referrals, or convert before consuming educational content.

When this occurs: Document the dominant path, show common alternate paths, identify loops and re-entry points, avoid presenting one path as universal, and explain what triggers movement between paths.

Use this format:

```text
Primary path:
Awareness → Problem recognition → Consideration → Conversion

Alternate path:
Referral → Evaluation → Sales conversation → Conversion

Loop:
Consideration ↔ Evaluation

Re-entry:
Past visitor → New trigger → Consideration
```

## Update workflow

When new customer or performance evidence is provided:

1. Identify the journey stage and touchpoint affected.
2. Compare the evidence with the current map.
3. Determine whether it confirms, contradicts, or expands the existing model.
4. Update the relevant stage, content need, or friction record.
5. Reassess downstream stages.
6. Update confidence.
7. Add a Change Log entry.
8. Identify affected campaigns, content, and channel plans.
9. Recommend validation when the change could affect major decisions.
