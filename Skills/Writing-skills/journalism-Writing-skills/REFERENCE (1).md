## Article Templates

### Template 1: Institutional News Analysis

```markdown
# [Institution] [Specific Decision]: What It Means for [Audience]

[Direct statement of what happened.]
[Immediate consequence and central tension.]

## The decision
[Report the verified action, timing, and responsible institution.]

## The official explanation
[Present the institution's position with precise attribution.]

## The practical effect
[Explain who experiences the decision and how.]

## The larger pressure
[Analyze finances, governance, regulation, competition, technology, politics, or audience behavior.]

## What the evidence shows
[Use documents, data, interviews, and independent context.]

## What remains unresolved
[Identify unanswered questions and disputed interpretations.]

## What happens next
[Explain deadlines, votes, investigations, implementation, or likely points of accountability.]
```

### Template 2: Media Industry Article

```markdown
# [Development] Shows How [Media Trend] Is Changing

[Lead with the newsroom, platform, ownership, labor, audience, or business development.]
[Introduce the tension between editorial purpose and institutional pressure.]

## The newsroom reality
[Show how the change affects daily work.]

## The business logic
[Explain revenue, costs, audience, ownership, or platform incentives.]

## What management says
[Attribute the official explanation.]
```

## Before-and-After Examples

### Institutional statement translation

**Source language:**
> We are undertaking a strategic transformation to optimize our operating model and enhance long-term value for stakeholders.

**Skill output:**
> The company is cutting costs and reorganizing its operations. Management says the changes will improve long-term performance, but the announcement does not specify how many jobs or services will be affected.

The rewrite translates the statement while preserving its uncertainty.

---

### News lead

**Weak:**
> The digital media industry is currently undergoing significant change.

**Stronger:**
> A new ownership group is reducing the staff of one of the country's largest digital newsrooms, a decision that could narrow coverage just as the publication is expanding into local reporting.

The stronger lead names the event, consequence, and tension.

---

### Personal observation

**Weak:**
> I found the entire announcement extremely frustrating.

**Stronger:**
> The announcement was 1,800 words long but never used the word "layoff."

The second version turns personal reaction into observable evidence.

---

### Data presentation

**Weak:**
> Usage increased by 32 percent.

**Stronger:**
> Monthly usage rose 32 percent between January and June, according to the company's internal figures. The data shows increased activity, but it does not show whether users stayed longer or simply opened the service more often.

---

### Official language analysis

**Example (correct):**
> The statement describes the layoffs as a "realignment." The word is not false, exactly, but it removes the people who will experience the change from the sentence.

This is analysis, not mind-reading. The wording itself is available evidence.

---

### Avoiding mind-reading

**Do not write:**
> The executive wanted to punish critics.

**Unless supported by strong evidence, write:**
> The decision had the effect of limiting critical coverage, though the executive described it as a cost-control measure.

---

### Wit vs. performance

**Good (clarifies contradiction):**
> The announcement promised simplification. It arrived in a 47-page document.

**Bad (performance, not reporting):**
> Apparently, the company's definition of simple is whatever requires a legal department.

## Institutional Analysis Framework (Complete)

### Power
- Who made the decision?
- Who could block it?
- Who had access to information?
- Who was not represented?
- Who controls the public narrative?

### Incentives
Revenue · Growth · Cost reduction · Regulatory compliance · Reputation · Political pressure · Career advancement · Audience retention · Investor expectations · Risk avoidance

### Capacity
Staff · Budget · Technology · Expertise · Time · Legal support · Organizational memory · Ability to execute

### Accountability
- Who is responsible?
- Is responsibility distributed or concentrated?
- Are there oversight mechanisms?
- Are promises measurable?
- Are consequences attached to failure?

### Communication
- What was announced? What was omitted?
- How quickly was it disclosed?
- Who received information first?
- Did the language change over time?
- Was the public statement consistent with internal documents?

### Consequences
Immediate · Financial · Editorial · Social · Legal · Operational · Long-term reputational

## Official Language Patterns to Watch

Look for these signals in institutional statements:

| Pattern | What it may conceal |
|---------|---------------------|
| Passive voice | Who made the decision |
| "Efficiency" | Cuts to staff or services |
| "Realignment" / "transformation" | Layoffs or restructuring |
| "Stakeholders" (unnamed) | Which interests were prioritized |
| "Moving forward" | Avoidance of what went wrong |
| "Community standards" | Enforcement decisions without criteria |
| "Operational changes" | Who is losing something |
| "Temporary adjustment" with no end date | Permanent change being soft-pedaled |
| "Voluntary" action | Action produced under pressure |
| Claims of inevitability | A choice being presented as a force of nature |

Translate carefully. Do not overstate intent.

## Source Hierarchy and Evaluation

**Order of preference:**
1. Primary documents
2. Directly observed evidence
3. On-the-record sources with relevant expertise
4. Independent experts
5. Public datasets
6. Reputable secondary reporting
7. Industry commentary
8. Verified social posts (used appropriately)
9. Anonymous sources (with justification and safeguards)

**Evaluate every source for:**
- Expertise and access
- Incentive
- Specificity
- Corroboration
- Timing
- Consistency with other evidence

A source's status or confidence does not make a claim true.

---

## Anonymous Source Standards

Use anonymous sources only when all of the following apply:

- The information is important
- The source has direct knowledge
- The source faces a meaningful risk
- The information cannot reasonably be obtained on the record
- The claim is independently corroborated when possible
- The reason for anonymity is explained without revealing the source

**Avoid vague descriptions:**
> A source familiar with the matter said...

**Prefer specificity:**
> A senior editor who reviewed the internal plan said the newsroom was given less than a week to implement it. The editor spoke anonymously because they were not authorized to discuss internal operations.

Do not use anonymity to conceal weak sourcing or ordinary opinion.

---

## Human Sources: Quality Standard

A human source in an institutional story should contribute one or more of: experience, knowledge, consequence, contradiction, specificity, or perspective.

Do not use people as decorative emotional evidence. Do not extract distress merely to intensify the article.

## Newsroom Details That Carry Weight

Small details that can explain large institutional changes:
- A changed headline
- A delayed investigation
- A staffing memo
- A correction pattern
- A shift in publishing frequency
- A new analytics dashboard
- A source protection decision
- A debate over whether a story should run
- A change in ownership or editorial leadership

## Document Analysis Checklist

When reading any document (report, memo, press release, filing, announcement), examine:

- [ ] Author and date
- [ ] Intended audience and purpose
- [ ] Definitions of key terms
- [ ] Scope and exceptions
- [ ] Enforcement mechanisms
- [ ] Missing information
- [ ] Changes from previous versions
- [ ] Terms that shift responsibility
- [ ] What the document does not address

Treat a document as evidence with a purpose, not a neutral record.

---

## Digital Presentation Standards

**Mobile-first principles:**
- Short paragraphs
- Strong headings
- Critical information near the top
- Lists for requirements or steps
- Plain-language summaries

**Scannable test:** A reader who reads only the headline, lead, headings, first sentence of each section, and bolded terms should still understand the article's basic argument.

**Search elements (generate when requested):**
- SEO headline
- Search title
- Meta description
- URL slug
- Primary and supporting search phrases
- Suggested internal links
- Social headline and description

Do not force search phrases into awkward sentences.

## Complete Pre-Delivery Checklist

### Reporting
- [ ] The main event or subject is clear in the lead
- [ ] The article identifies who, what, when, where, and why
- [ ] Every major claim has a source or is clearly labeled as analysis
- [ ] Official statements are attributed
- [ ] Contradictions are demonstrated, not merely asserted
- [ ] The timeline is accurate
- [ ] Important uncertainty is visible

### Voice
- [ ] The language is conversational but not casual
- [ ] The authority comes from reporting, not exaggeration
- [ ] Personal voice is limited to useful observations
- [ ] Humor, if used, is restrained
- [ ] The article does not imitate a specific writer's signature style
- [ ] The tone respects readers' intelligence

### Institutional analysis
- [ ] Incentives and constraints are explained
- [ ] Power and accountability are visible
- [ ] Official language is examined carefully
- [ ] Human consequences are included where relevant
- [ ] The institution is not reduced to a single villain or hero

### Digital readability
- [ ] The headline is descriptive
- [ ] Sections are easy to scan
- [ ] Paragraphs are manageable
- [ ] Technical concepts are explained
- [ ] Links are useful and descriptive
- [ ] The article works on mobile screens
- [ ] The reader knows what happens next

## Prohibited Patterns

The skill must not:

- Reconstruct recognizable passages from any specific writer
- Mimic a distinctive personal manner closely enough to constitute imitation
- Present invention as reporting
- Fabricate interviews, documents, or statistics
- Attribute opinions to unnamed experts without justification
- Use sarcasm as a substitute for evidence
- Hide uncertainty behind confident prose
- Treat institutional statements as verified fact
- Turn a person into a symbol without reporting their actual experience
- Use personal voice to dominate a reported story
- Add background unrelated to the reader's question
- End with a generic conclusion that merely repeats the article
- Make the writer's frustration or admiration the story

---

## Transitions Reference

Use transitions to show reasoning, not to decorate paragraphs:

- "That is the official version."
- "The timeline, however, is more complicated."
- "The numbers tell a different story."
- "The practical effect is easier to see on the ground."
- "This is where the institutional question begins."
- "The decision did not come out of nowhere."
- "What remains unclear is..."
- "The next test will be..."

Avoid repeating the same transition pattern throughout one article.

---

## Corrections and Transparency

Handle errors and uncertainty directly:

- *"An earlier version of this article misstated..."*
- *"This article has been updated to include..."*
- *"The original announcement did not specify..."*
- *"The data was revised after publication..."*

If reporting is incomplete, say so:
> This article is based on the public documents available as of [date]. The organization has not provided the underlying data.

Do not quietly preserve a known error.
