# Claude Skill: Web Article Architect

## Skill Purpose

This skill transforms research, notes, source material, announcements, reports, interviews, and expert input into clear, credible, scannable web articles.

It combines:

- Digital journalism.
- Online publishing.
- Content marketing.
- Web usability.
- Search-friendly information architecture.
- Evidence-based writing.
- Reader-oriented calls to action.

The skill does not impose one universal voice. It adapts the article’s structure, tone, depth, and persuasive intensity to the subject, audience, publication, and reader intent.

***

## Core Editorial Principle

A strong web article should generally move from the most important information to the supporting detail:

> **Descriptive headline → direct lead → key facts → supporting evidence → clear sections → useful links → context → reader action or next step**

This structure should remain flexible. Breaking news may require a strict inverted pyramid. An analytical article may delay the central interpretation to create a carefully developed argument. A practical guide may prioritize the reader’s task over chronology.

The skill must always answer:

1. What is this article about?
2. Why does it matter?
3. Who is affected?
4. What is known?
5. What evidence supports it?
6. What remains uncertain?
7. What should the reader do next?

***

# Operating Instructions

When given a topic or source material, perform the following workflow.

## Step 1: Identify the Assignment

Determine:

- The subject.
- The article type.
- The intended audience.
- The reader’s likely question.
- The publication or channel.
- The desired tone.
- The required length.
- The primary objective.
- The expected reader action.
- Any legal, regulatory, political, medical, financial, or reputational sensitivity.

If the user has not provided enough information, make reasonable assumptions and state them briefly before drafting.

Do not ask unnecessary questions when a useful draft can be produced immediately.

***

## Step 2: Classify the Article

Choose the dominant format.

### News article

Prioritize:

- What happened.
- Who is involved.
- When it happened.
- Where it happened.
- Why it matters.
- What happens next.

Use a direct lead and inverted-pyramid structure.

### Explainer

Prioritize:

- Definition.
- Background.
- Mechanism.
- Effects.
- Examples.
- Limitations.
- Practical implications.

Use clear questions as section headings where useful.

### How-to guide

Prioritize:

- The reader’s goal.
- Required preparation.
- Sequential steps.
- Warnings.
- Examples.
- Troubleshooting.
- Completion criteria.

Use numbered steps and imperative language.

### Analysis

Prioritize:

- A clear thesis.
- Relevant evidence.
- Competing interpretations.
- Causal reasoning.
- Implications.
- Uncertainty.

Distinguish facts from interpretation.

### Opinion or commentary

Prioritize:

- A clearly identified viewpoint.
- Fair representation of opposing arguments.
- Original reasoning.
- Concrete examples.
- A defensible conclusion.

Do not present opinions as neutral facts.

### Case study

Prioritize:

- Initial situation.
- Problem.
- Intervention.
- Evidence.
- Results.
- Limitations.
- Transferable lessons.

Avoid unsupported claims of causation.

### Service journalism

Prioritize:

- Immediate usefulness.
- Eligibility.
- Cost.
- Timing.
- Requirements.
- Risks.
- Practical next steps.

Put the information readers need to act near the top.

### Brand or thought-leadership article

Prioritize:

- Reader value before brand promotion.
- An original point of view.
- Evidence or experience.
- Useful examples.
- Appropriate product or organizational relevance.

Avoid turning the article into an advertisement.

***

## Step 3: Define the Central Point

Write a one-sentence editorial thesis before drafting:

> This article explains **[what happened or what the subject means]**, why it matters to **[specific audience]**, and what they can do next.

If the topic is news-driven, use:

> **[Organization, event, or development]** is changing **[specific situation]** by **[main consequence]**, affecting **[audience]**.

If the topic is educational, use:

> **[Subject]** works by **[simple explanation]**, but its usefulness depends on **[important condition or limitation]**.

If the central point cannot be expressed clearly, do not begin with decorative prose. Clarify the subject first.

***

# Headline System

## Headline Objective

The headline should communicate the article’s subject and value quickly.

A strong headline is:

- Descriptive.
- Specific.
- Accurate.
- Useful.
- Understandable without context.
- Appropriate for search and social sharing.
- Free from unnecessary exaggeration.

Avoid:

- Vague wordplay.
- Empty superlatives.
- Artificial urgency.
- Unexplained acronyms.
- Clickbait.
- Claims stronger than the evidence.
- Headlines that conceal the subject.


## Headline Templates

### News

- **[Organization] Announces [Specific Change]**
- **[Development] Will Affect [Audience] Starting [Date]**
- **What [Event or Decision] Means for [Audience]**
- **[Policy, Product, or Rule] Changes How [Activity] Works**


### Explainer

- **What Is [Subject] and Why Does It Matter?**
- **How [System or Process] Works**
- **A Practical Guide to [Subject]**
- **What We Know About [Topic]**


### Analysis

- **Why [Development] Matters Beyond [Immediate Event]**
- **The Strategic Meaning of [Development]**
- **What [Trend] Reveals About [Larger Issue]**


### Service article

- **How to [Desired Outcome]**
- **What You Need to Know Before [Action]**
- **[Number] Steps to [Outcome]**
- **Requirements, Costs, and Timelines for [Action]**


## Headline Quality Test

Ask:

1. Can a new reader identify the subject?
2. Does the headline match the article’s actual content?
3. Is the main benefit or consequence clear?
4. Does it avoid promising more than the article delivers?
5. Could it be made more precise by adding an audience, date, location, or consequence?

***

# Direct Lead System

## Lead Objective

The lead should deliver the article’s main point immediately.

A direct lead usually answers the most important version of:

- What happened?
- What changed?
- Why does it matter?
- Who needs to pay attention?
- What can the reader learn or do?

Do not begin with:

- A generic statement.
- A rhetorical question.
- A dictionary definition unless necessary.
- A long historical background.
- A quotation without context.
- A dramatic claim unsupported by evidence.
- A personal anecdote that delays the point.
- Several paragraphs of scene-setting before explaining the subject.


## Lead Templates

### News lead

> **[Entity]** has **[announced, introduced, changed, released, or faced]** **[specific development]**, affecting **[audience or area]** from **[date or timeframe]**.

### Explainer lead

> **[Subject]** is **[plain-language definition]**. It matters because **[practical consequence or relevance]**.

### Analysis lead

> **[Development]** appears to mark **[larger significance]**, although its impact will depend on **[condition or uncertainty]**.

### How-to lead

> To **[achieve outcome]**, you need to **[main approach]**. The process involves **[number or broad sequence of steps]**.

### Content marketing lead

> **[Audience]** often struggles with **[problem]**. A more effective approach is to **[useful principle]**, because **[reason]**.

## Lead Length

Prefer one concise paragraph. Use two short paragraphs only when the subject requires immediate qualification, such as an important limitation, date, location, or uncertainty.

***

# Key Facts Layer

After the lead, present the facts readers need most.

Use a short list, compact paragraph, or “at a glance” box when appropriate.

## Key Facts May Include

- Date.
- Location.
- People or organizations involved.
- Scale.
- Cost.
- Eligibility.
- Deadline.
- Main change.
- Expected effect.
- Source of authority.
- Current status.
- Important limitation.

Do not repeat the lead word for word. Add information that helps the reader understand or act.

## Fact Priority

Rank facts by:

1. Relevance to the reader.
2. Consequence.
3. Timeliness.
4. Certainty.
5. Specificity.

A fact should not appear first merely because it is interesting. It should appear first because it helps the reader understand the subject.

***

# Evidence and Attribution

## Evidence Standard

Every significant factual claim should be:

- Supported by a reliable source.
- Attributed clearly.
- Separated from opinion.
- Presented with appropriate uncertainty.
- Relevant to the claim being made.

Use primary sources whenever possible:

- Official documents.
- Regulatory filings.
- Court records.
- Research papers.
- Company announcements.
- Government datasets.
- Direct interviews.
- Original surveys.
- Public statements.
- Technical documentation.

Use secondary sources for:

- Independent context.
- Expert interpretation.
- Historical background.
- Comparative reporting.
- Additional verification.


## Attribution Language

Use precise verbs:

- “The report states…”
- “The agency announced…”
- “Researchers found…”
- “The filing shows…”
- “According to the company…”
- “Experts interviewed for this article said…”
- “Available data suggests…”
- “The evidence does not yet establish…”

Avoid vague attribution:

- “People say…”
- “Experts believe…” without naming them.
- “Studies show…” without identifying the study.
- “It is widely known…”
- “Many are concerned…” without evidence.


## Separate Fact and Interpretation

Use labels or explicit transitions:

- **Fact:** The regulation takes effect on 1 January.
- **Interpretation:** The practical impact may be greater for small publishers because they have fewer compliance resources.
- **Uncertainty:** It remains unclear how enforcement will treat borderline cases.

Do not convert an interpretation into a fact through confident wording.

***

# Web Article Structure

Use this default structure when appropriate:

```markdown
# Descriptive headline

Direct lead explaining the main point.

## Key facts

- Fact one.
- Fact two.
- Fact three.

## Who is affected

Explain the relevant audience, organizations, communities, or stakeholders.

## What changes

Describe the new development, requirement, finding, or process.

## Why it matters

Explain the practical, social, commercial, legal, or strategic significance.

## What the evidence shows

Present the strongest supporting sources and identify limitations.

## How it works in practice

Use examples, steps, scenarios, or implementation details.

## What remains unclear

Identify unanswered questions, disputed claims, data gaps, or future decisions.

## What readers should do next

Give practical, proportionate, and clearly prioritized actions.

## Sources and useful links

Link to original documents, authoritative resources, relevant tools, or further reading.
```

Do not use every section automatically. Remove sections that do not serve the reader.

***

# Section Design

## Section Headings

Use headings that describe the information below them.

Good headings:

- Who is affected?
- What changes?
- How does the process work?
- What does the data show?
- What remains uncertain?
- What should publishers do now?

Weak headings:

- The Bigger Picture.
- A New Era.
- What You Need to Know, when the section does not specify what the reader will learn.
- Final Thoughts.
- Moving Forward.

A section heading should help a scanning reader decide whether to continue.

## Section Length

Keep sections focused. A section should usually develop one idea or answer one question.

Break up long sections when:

- The subject changes.
- The reader’s question changes.
- A new type of evidence begins.
- The content becomes difficult to scan.
- A section exceeds a reasonable screenful for the intended audience.


## Paragraph Length

Prefer short paragraphs for web reading:

- One main idea per paragraph.
- Usually one to three sentences.
- Begin with the point, then provide explanation.
- Avoid dense blocks of text.
- Vary paragraph length naturally.

***

# Scannability Rules

Readers often scan online content before deciding whether to read closely. Design the article so that the main argument remains understandable through headings, opening sentences, lists, links, and emphasis.

Use:

- Descriptive subheadings.
- Short paragraphs.
- Bulleted lists.
- Numbered steps.
- Bold emphasis for genuinely important terms.
- Tables for multidimensional comparisons.
- Pull quotes only when they add meaning.
- Examples after abstract explanations.
- Summary sentences at the end of complex sections.

Avoid:

- Formatting every sentence.
- Long introductory passages.
- Unexplained technical terminology.
- Repeating the same point in multiple formats.
- Excessive nested lists.
- Large blocks of bold text.
- Overuse of rhetorical questions.
- Decorative headings that hide the subject.


## First-Sentence Rule

The first sentence of each section should communicate the section’s primary point whenever possible.

***

# Clarity and Precision

## Use Plain Language

Prefer:

- “use” instead of “utilize.”
- “help” instead of “facilitate.”
- “before” instead of “prior to.”
- “because” instead of “due to the fact that.”
- “about” instead of “with regard to.”

Do not simplify away necessary precision.

## Prefer Concrete Language

Weak:

> The initiative could potentially have a significant impact on a wide range of stakeholders.

Stronger:

> The initiative could increase reporting costs for small publishers.

## Use Specific Verbs

Prefer:

- “requires.”
- “reduces.”
- “increases.”
- “limits.”
- “allows.”
- “prohibits.”
- “funds.”
- “delays.”
- “replaces.”
- “affects.”

Avoid relying heavily on:

- “is.”
- “does.”
- “makes.”
- “provides.”
- “addresses.”
- “leverages.”
- “impacts.”


## Control Adjectives

Use adjectives only when they add factual meaning.

Avoid unverified terms such as:

- Revolutionary.
- Game-changing.
- Unprecedented.
- Leading.
- Innovative.
- Massive.
- Groundbreaking.
- Essential.

If such language appears in a source, attribute it rather than adopting it as fact.

***

# The Inverted Pyramid

Use the inverted pyramid when readers need the answer quickly.

## Order

1. Most important development.
2. Immediate consequence.
3. Key supporting facts.
4. Evidence and attribution.
5. Relevant context.
6. Secondary details.
7. Future implications.

This structure is especially appropriate for:

- News.
- Product announcements.
- Regulatory updates.
- Crisis communication.
- Research findings.
- Public notices.
- Business developments.


## When Not to Use It Rigidly

A strict inverted pyramid may be unsuitable when the article’s purpose is:

- Narrative storytelling.
- Personal essay.
- Investigative reconstruction.
- Literary criticism.
- Brand storytelling.
- A complex tutorial.
- A carefully developed analytical argument.

Even then, provide early orientation so readers know the subject and stakes.

***

# Context System

Context should clarify the current development, not bury it.

Include context when it explains:

- Why the issue emerged.
- What happened previously.
- Which law, policy, product, or event is relevant.
- How the current situation compares with earlier conditions.
- Why different stakeholders disagree.
- What readers may misunderstand.

Avoid:

- Historical background unrelated to the article’s point.
- Chronologies that delay the main information.
- Context presented without dates.
- Old statistics presented as current.
- Background that repeats information already established.

Use dates and time markers carefully:

- “In March 2025…”
- “Since the policy took effect…”
- “The latest available data, published in June 2026…”
- “As of August 2026…”

Do not use “recently,” “currently,” or “soon” when a specific date is available.

***

# Links and Source Design

## Link Objective

Useful links allow readers to verify claims, explore primary material, or take the next step.

Prioritize links to:

- Original reports.
- Official regulations.
- Research papers.
- Public datasets.
- Documentation.
- Relevant tools.
- Application pages.
- Earlier articles that provide necessary context.


## Link Text

Use descriptive anchor text.

Good:

- “Read the regulator’s final guidance.”
- “Review the publisher’s moderation policy.”
- “Download the complete dataset.”

Weak:

- “Click here.”
- “Learn more.”
- “This link.”
- “Read more.”

Do not insert links merely to increase link count. Each link should serve a clear reader purpose.

## Link Placement

Place a link close to the claim or action it supports.

For important claims, link directly to the strongest relevant source. Do not force readers to search within a homepage for the cited information.

***

# Reader Action and Next Step

Every article should provide an appropriate next step when one exists.

The next step may be:

- Read the original document.
- Check eligibility.
- Compare options.
- Subscribe to updates.
- Contact an authority.
- Review a policy.
- Complete a checklist.
- Test a method.
- Monitor a deadline.
- Consult a qualified professional.
- Continue to a related guide.

The action must match the article’s purpose and the reader’s level of intent.

## Action Quality Test

A useful action is:

- Specific.
- Feasible.
- Proportionate.
- Clearly connected to the article.
- Easy to locate.
- Free from unnecessary pressure.

Avoid vague endings such as:

- “Stay tuned.”
- “The future will tell.”
- “What do you think?”
- “Contact us today” without explaining why.
- “Take action now” without a concrete action.

***

# Search and Discoverability

## Search-Friendly Writing

Optimize for clarity before keywords.

Use the likely reader language in:

- Headline.
- Introduction.
- Relevant section headings.
- Image descriptions.
- Link text.
- Page title.
- Meta description.
- FAQ section, when genuinely useful.

Do not repeat keywords unnaturally.

## Search Intent

Identify the dominant intent:

- Informational: “What is this?”
- Navigational: “Where can I find this?”
- Transactional: “How do I buy, apply, or subscribe?”
- Investigative: “Which option is best?”
- Practical: “How do I do this?”
- News-oriented: “What happened and what does it mean?”

Make the opening and structure match that intent.

## Search Snippet Principle

The headline and opening paragraph should make sense when displayed without the rest of the article.

***

# Content Marketing Adaptation

When writing for marketing, maintain editorial usefulness.

## Value-First Rule

The article should help the reader even if they do not buy, subscribe, register, or contact the organization.

Include:

- A real audience problem.
- A useful point of view.
- Evidence or experience.
- Practical examples.
- Clear distinctions.
- Honest limitations.
- A relevant but restrained brand connection.


## Product Mention Rules

Mention a product, service, or company when it:

- Directly solves a problem explained in the article.
- Provides evidence for a claim.
- Represents a relevant example.
- Helps the reader complete the next step.

Avoid:

- Inserting the brand into every section.
- Unsupported superiority claims.
- Competitor disparagement.
- Promotional language disguised as neutral reporting.
- Treating testimonials as independent evidence.

***

# Voice and Style Modes

The skill can use different writing modes.

## Objective News Mode

Characteristics:

- Direct.
- Neutral.
- Fact-led.
- Attributed.
- Chronological only when useful.
- Low emotional intensity.


## Practical Explainer Mode

Characteristics:

- Clear.
- Instructional.
- Reader-centered.
- Definition-led.
- Example-rich.
- Explicit about limitations.


## Expert Analysis Mode

Characteristics:

- Analytical.
- Precise.
- Evidence-based.
- Comfortable with nuance.
- Clear about assumptions and uncertainty.


## Editorial Commentary Mode

Characteristics:

- Distinctive voice.
- Strong thesis.
- Persuasive reasoning.
- Fair treatment of alternatives.
- Explicit opinion.


## Brand Thought-Leadership Mode

Characteristics:

- Useful before promotional.
- Point-of-view-driven.
- Human and confident.
- Supported by evidence.
- Connected to organizational expertise without sounding like a sales page.


## Conversion Copy Mode

Characteristics:

- Audience-specific.
- Benefit-oriented.
- Concrete.
- Emotionally relevant.
- Action-focused.
- Tested against reader objections.

Do not blend modes accidentally. If a piece needs both objectivity and persuasion, separate factual reporting from the organization’s interpretation.

***

# Accuracy and Risk Controls

## Claims Audit

Before finalizing, identify each important claim and classify it as:

- Verified fact.
- Attributed statement.
- Reasonable interpretation.
- Forecast.
- Opinion.
- Example.
- Recommendation.
- Unverified claim.

Revise any claim whose category is unclear.

## Uncertainty Language

Use calibrated language:

- “The evidence shows…”
- “The available data indicates…”
- “The report estimates…”
- “Researchers found an association, not necessarily causation.”
- “It remains unclear…”
- “The policy does not specify…”
- “The company claims…”
- “One possible explanation is…”

Avoid false certainty.

## Sensitive Topics

For medical, legal, financial, political, safety, or reputational topics:

- Use authoritative sources.
- Include dates.
- Distinguish general information from professional advice.
- Avoid unsupported predictions.
- Explain relevant limitations.
- Avoid sensational language.
- Attribute allegations.
- Give the subject an opportunity to respond when appropriate.
- Do not imply guilt from an accusation.
- Do not overstate preliminary findings.

***

# Examples and Illustrations

Use examples to clarify, not decorate.

## Abstract-to-Concrete Pattern

1. State the principle.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Show a short example.
4. Identify how the reader can apply it.

### Example

> A web headline should describe the consequence, not merely name the event.
>
> Weak: **New Publishing Rules Announced**
>
> Stronger: **New Publishing Rules Require Independent Websites to Explain Complaint Procedures**

The second headline gives readers the subject and the practical consequence immediately.

## Before-and-After Editing

### Weak

> In the current rapidly changing digital environment, publishers are increasingly being required to consider a variety of new responsibilities relating to safety and transparency.

### Stronger

> New rules may require publishers to explain how they moderate harmful content and handle user complaints.

The revised version uses a concrete subject, an active verb, and a specific consequence.

***

# Reusable Article Blueprint

Use this blueprint for most substantial web articles:

```markdown
# [Descriptive headline]

[Direct lead: explain the main development, answer, or benefit in one or two sentences.]

## Key facts

- [Most important fact]
- [Who or what is affected]
- [Date, scale, cost, requirement, or status]
- [Important limitation or uncertainty]

## [Reader-centered question or topic]

[Explain the first major aspect. Lead with the answer.]

## [Second reader-centered question or topic]

[Explain what changes, how it works, or why it matters.]

## Evidence and sources

[Present the strongest evidence. Attribute each important claim and distinguish reported facts from interpretation.]

## Context

[Explain only the background necessary to understand the current subject.]

## What remains unclear

[Identify unresolved questions, disputed claims, limitations, or future decisions.]

## What readers can do next

1. [Immediate action]
2. [Verification or preparation step]
3. [Follow-up action]

## Useful links

- [Descriptive link to primary source]
- [Descriptive link to relevant guidance]
- [Descriptive link to related resource]
```


***

# Editing Passes

Perform the following passes in order.

## Pass 1: Accuracy

Check:

- Names.
- Dates.
- Numbers.
- Titles.
- Organizations.
- Quotations.
- Source relevance.
- Claim strength.
- Current status.


## Pass 2: Structure

Check:

- The main point appears early.
- Sections follow reader logic.
- Important facts are not buried.
- Context appears after orientation.
- The next step is clear.


## Pass 3: Clarity

Check:

- Sentences have clear subjects and verbs.
- Technical terms are explained.
- Pronouns have clear references.
- Abstract language is replaced with concrete language.
- Unnecessary qualifications are removed.


## Pass 4: Scannability

Check:

- Headings describe the content.
- Paragraphs are manageable.
- Lists are used where useful.
- Key information can be found quickly.
- No section contains multiple unrelated ideas.


## Pass 5: Tone

Check:

- The voice matches the assignment.
- The article does not sound inflated.
- Promotional claims are restrained.
- Opinion is labeled.
- Uncertainty is neither hidden nor exaggerated.


## Pass 6: Action

Check:

- The article answers “what now?”
- The recommended action is realistic.
- Links support verification or action.
- The ending does not merely repeat the introduction.

***

# Final Quality Checklist

Before delivering the article, verify:

### Editorial

- [ ] The article has one clear central point.
- [ ] The headline accurately reflects the article.
- [ ] The lead communicates the main point directly.
- [ ] The intended audience is clear.
- [ ] Facts and opinions are separated.
- [ ] The article does not claim more than the evidence supports.


### Web usability

- [ ] Sections are easy to scan.
- [ ] Headings are descriptive.
- [ ] Paragraphs are concise.
- [ ] Lists appear where they improve comprehension.
- [ ] Technical language is explained.
- [ ] Important information is not hidden in the conclusion.


### Evidence

- [ ] Significant claims are attributed.
- [ ] Primary sources are prioritized.
- [ ] Dates and numbers are checked.
- [ ] Uncertainty is stated where relevant.
- [ ] Links are useful and descriptive.
- [ ] Promotional claims are identified or qualified.


### Reader value

- [ ] The article answers the reader’s likely questions.
- [ ] Examples clarify complex ideas.
- [ ] Context is relevant and proportionate.
- [ ] The reader knows what to do next.
- [ ] The article remains useful without requiring a purchase or conversion.

***

# Output Instructions for Claude

When generating an article, return the following unless the user requests another format:

1. **Editorial angle:** One sentence describing the article’s central point.
2. **Recommended headline:** One primary headline and, when useful, two alternatives.
3. **Article draft:** Fully structured with a direct lead, clear headings, evidence, context, and next step.
4. **Source notes:** Identify which claims require verification or stronger sourcing.
5. **Reader action:** State the most useful next step.
6. **Editorial quality notes:** Briefly flag ambiguity, uncertainty, promotional risk, missing data, or unresolved questions.

When the user provides sources, do not invent facts that are absent from those sources. Mark unsupported claims as needing verification.

When the user asks for a concise article, preserve the same logic while reducing supporting detail. When the user asks for a long-form article, expand context, evidence, examples, counterarguments, and practical implications without repeating the main point.

The final article should be **clear before clever, useful before promotional, specific before broad, and accurate before persuasive**.
